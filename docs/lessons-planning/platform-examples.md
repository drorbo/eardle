# Platform Structural Examples (Research Draft)

Short, paraphrased summaries of how real ear-training / music-theory
platforms organize content and navigation — structure and hierarchy only,
not their specific lesson content.

## musictheory.net

Splits into two clearly separate top-level sections — "Lessons" (39
short animated explainer pages) and "Exercises" / "Trainers" (drill
tools) — that are cross-linked but not forced into a single linear path.
A learner can jump straight to a drill with zero login and zero setup, or
read a lesson first; the site doesn't gate exercises behind lessons. There
is also a separate teacher-facing "custom exercise creator" for assembling
targeted drills, showing a structural split between the general public
content tree and instructor-assigned practice.

## teoria.com

Organizes material as a structured "course tree" of theory topics, with a
distinct, separately-listed "Exercises" area (documented via its own
help page) offering several categories of drills. Compared to
musictheory.net's flatter two-bucket model, teoria.com leans more into a
hierarchical course structure for the lesson side, while still keeping
exercises as an adjacent, independently browsable section rather than
embedded inline in every lesson page.

## Musicca

Presents "Lessons" and "Exercises" as parallel top-level sections (mirroring
musictheory.net's split) but adds account-based progress tracking and
lightweight gamification (saved results, competing with other learners) on
top. Lessons are explicitly framed as short/"bite-sized" with instant
feedback, and the site supports starting from any topic rather than
enforcing one fixed order — structurally closer to a topic library than a
mandatory course sequence.

## Hooktheory (TheoryTab)

A fundamentally different structural model from the three above: instead
of an abstract lesson tree, content is organized around a large searchable
database of real songs, each annotated with its underlying roman-numeral
chord progression and scale-degree-anchored melody. Navigation is
song-first or progression-first (search an artist/song, or browse all
songs sharing a given progression) rather than topic-first — theory
concepts are learned as annotations layered onto music the learner already
knows, rather than as standalone lesson pages.

## EarMaster

Uses an explicit two-level hierarchy: **Modules** (thematic groupings,
e.g. by difficulty or curriculum stage) containing **Lessons** (the
actual training units, each either pure explanation, pure drill, or a mix
of both). Every lesson opens with a two-pane "Lesson Introduction" —
description text on one side, audio/visual examples on the other — before
dropping into the drill itself. A persistent color-coded progress strip
(grey/blue/green/red for upcoming/current/correct/incorrect questions)
shows the learner's position within the current lesson at all times. This
is the most "coursebook-like" structure among sources surveyed — clearly
designed for teachers assigning sequenced modules to students, not just
free browsing.

## Complete Ear Trainer

Offers two parallel tracks at different depths rather than one fixed
path: an "Easy Mode" (beginner-oriented, ~12 chapters) and a "Classic
Mode" (~28 chapters, 4 difficulty levels, 150+ drills) covering the same
broad topic space (intervals, chords, scales, progressions, dictation) at
different granularity. Progression within each track is drill-by-drill
inside numbered chapters, gated so a topic must be mastered before the
next unlocks — a stricter "must complete to advance" structure than the
browse-anything model of musictheory.net/Musicca.

## College aural-skills courses (general pattern, not one specific school)

Typically span multiple semesters, run as a companion course alongside a
separate written-theory course covering the same topics in the same term.
Early terms are described as covering "aural fundamentals" broadly; later
terms progressively add sight-reading, dictation, improvisation/"playback,"
transcription, and ensemble skills, with basic form- and chord-listening
work reserved for the final terms. Structurally, this is the most rigidly
linear/gated model of all sources surveyed — a cohort progresses together
term by term rather than a learner choosing their own order.

## Cross-platform pattern summary

- **Two common navigational shapes**: (1) a lesson-library-plus-adjacent-
  drills model where topics can be browsed in any order (musictheory.net,
  Musicca, teoria.com), and (2) a gated module/chapter model requiring
  mastery before advancing (EarMaster, Complete Ear Trainer, college
  courses).
- **Progress display** is near-universal in the gated/app-style tools
  (color-coded strips, chapter completion, saved scores) but far less
  emphasized on the free browse-style sites.
- **Song-first navigation (Hooktheory)** stands apart as the one model
  organized around real music rather than an abstract topic tree — a
  useful alternate framing if a "connect theory back to real songs" angle
  is ever wanted alongside a more conventional lesson tree.
